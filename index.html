Abstract

The Manhattan project was a program carried out during World War II that strived to create the first atomic bomb. More specifically, it was administered by the United States of America in order to create atomic weapons before the Germans could. Led by J. Robert Oppenheimer and General Leslie Groves, the Manhattan Project begun in August of 1942, although Oppenheimer would not be assigned to the project until October of that same year. The program was conducted across multiple key locations over the duration of the war, with significant ones being Los Alamos, Oak Ridge and Hanford, which are in New Mexico, Tennessee and Washington respectively. Over the course of the program, many monumental breakthroughs were achieved, such as nuclear fission, plutonium synthesis, uranium enrichment and implosion devices. These were all necessary in order to build the atomic bomb, but have since proved useful for many other purposes as well. The culmination of the program was the Trinity Test in July of 1945, which was a first time success.  Two atomic bombs would later be dropped on Japan in August of the same year just days apart. Less than a week later, Japan would surrender, marking the end of the second World War.

Introduction

Prior to WWII, in December 1938, two German Chemists, Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann discovered that bombarding uranium atoms with neutrons would cause the uranium to absorb the neutrons and split into two lighter elements, specifically barium, whilst also releasing immense amounts of energy and simultaneously releasing extra neutrons in the process. This brought the realisation that this process could create a chain reaction which would continuously release large amounts of energy. This process would later be called nuclear fission (Meitner and Frisch, 1939). The discovery of nuclear fission was brought to America by Niels Bohr, and a team of scientists at Columbia were able to successfully replicate it in January of 1939. The team was led by John R. Dunning and included Herbert L. Anderson,  Eugene T. Booth, G. Norris Glasoe, Francis G. Slack and Enrico Fermi.

Analysis and discussion

After fission was discovered, scientists realized that the large energy releases and the nature of the chain reaction would theoretically be able to create a bomb with enormous explosive power. Fearing that a war was in the near future and the Nazis had also realised this, German scientist Leo Szilard thought to warn the United States of the discovery. In August 1939, less than a month before the start of the second World War, a letter was written by Leo Szilard and signed by Albert Einstein to inform the President at the time, Franklin D. Roosevelt, that the Nazis were working to develop atomic weapons. This led to the U.S. government investing in nuclear research (Rhodes, 1986). Later, in August of 1942, the U.S. Army Corps of engineers established the Manhattan Engineer District (MED), putting the project under full military control. In September, General Leslie Groves was put in charge of the program, which then became a full scale industrial operation (Hewlett and Anderson, 1962). General Groves decided to give J. Robert Oppenheimer the role of Scientific Director of the project in October that same year. In order to complete the project as quick as possible, Oppenheimer suggested to build a laboratory in Los Alamos, a remote desert in New Mexico, as this would allow the project to stay confidential and also would keep all of the personnel working on the project in the same location at all times. This decision was approved by the U.S. government in February 1943, formalising Oppenheimer’s role in the program. In July 1943, General Groves issued a letter of Oppenheimer’s appointment as the Director of the Los Alamos Laboratory, giving him full control of the operations taking place on the site (Bird and Sherwin, 2005) (Groves 1962). Including Los Alamos, three main sites were used in the development of the bomb. The first was Oak Ridge, Tennessee (Site X) which focused on industrial scale isotope separation to produce enriched uranium-235. This was achieved via methods such as electromagnetic separation and thermal diffusion. The next location was Hanford, Washington (Site W) which used large scale nuclear reactors to synthesise plutonium-239. The final location was Los Alamos, New Mexico (Site Y) which was used as the central scientific laboratory under J. Robert Oppenheimer to actually design and assemble the weapons. It was also where the Trinity Test would later be carried out (Hewlett and Anderson, 1962). In the summer of 1944, researchers realised that the Oak Ridge site was producing enriched uranium far too slowly and they would only be able to build one bomb with it. This caused the focus to shift onto the plutonium bomb. However, the detonation mechanism that would be used on the uranium bomb would not work on the plutonium bomb as it would detonate prematurely, so the scientists had to develop an implosion device. The device would compress the plutonium sphere inward until it reached critical mass, instead of firing the spheres at each other, which was used for the uranium bomb (Hoddeson et al., 1993). On the 16th of July 1945 at 5:29am, the first ever nuclear weapon was detonated. This was called the Trinity Test. Since there was only enough uranium for one bomb, only the plutonium bomb was able to be tested. The bomb detonated and produced energy equal to 21 kilotons of TNT, which is 88 trillion joules, far exceeding the initial estimates. The explosion was visible for over 200 miles, the heat wave was felt tens of miles away, and a cloud was formed that reached over 40,000 feet into the atmosphere. The heat from the explosion turned the sand in the desert into a glassy mineral called trinitite. The success of the test allowed for the bomb to be used for military purposes (Rhodes, 1986). Since all other forces has already surrendered by the time the bombs were ready for use, two were dropped on Japan. The uranium bomb, called ‘Little Boy’, was dropped on Hiroshima on the 6th of August 1945, immediately killing over 70,000 people, and killing over 140,000 in the long term. The plutonium bomb, named ‘Fat Man’, was later dropped on Nagasaki on the 9th of August 1945, which immediately killed over 35,000 people, and over 70,000 more in the long term. Japan would then surrender shortly after on the 2nd of September, marking the end of the second World War.

Conclusion

To conclude, the Manhattan Project was technically very successful in the sense that the bomb successfully detonated first time and it managed to bring an end to the war, however, the scientists involved in the project, especially Oppenheimer, 
were faced with many moral qualms due to the many innocent lives lost in the process, and the potential for nuclear war being discovered.
